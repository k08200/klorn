SaneBox reads the headers.
Klorn reads the message.

SaneBox has been doing folder-based triage since 2010 and does it without an app of its own. The trade it makes is deliberate: it looks at sender, subject and date, never the message body — so it can be private in a way Klorn is not, and it cannot tell a scheduling request from a newsletter that mentions a date.

The honest part: SaneBox has the stronger privacy sentence

“We never read your mail” is one sentence, it needs no qualifier, and Klorn cannot say it. Sorting mail into a lane with a stated reason requires reading the message. If header-only filtering is enough for you, that is a real advantage and we are not going to argue it away.

What Klorn offers instead is structural rather than rhetorical: the whole stack is AGPL-3.0 and self-hostable, you can point it at your own model key or a local model so message text never leaves your machine, and every classification is bound to a hash of the exact bytes that produced it. The short version is that you can own the server that reads your mail — a longer sentence than SaneBox's, and a checkable one.

What reading the body buys

Header signals cannot tell a scheduling request from a newsletter that mentions a date. Klorn routes scheduling mail into its own lane and cross-checks it against your calendar — the proposed slot, whether it clashes, and the first slots verified free for both sides. That is not a difference of quality; it is a difference of what information the system is allowed to see.

What we are not claiming. No head-to-head accuracy test between Klorn and SaneBox has been run. SaneBox's approach is described here as the vendor describes it publicly.

SaneBox is also the counter-example worth respecting: bootstrapped since 2010, profitable, and running on a fraction of the surface area. Being open source does not make Klorn better at what SaneBox is good at.
